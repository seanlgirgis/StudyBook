1. Big Picture

Database design begins before tables are created. First identify how the business will read, write, and analyze data. Operational workloads need fast, reliable transactions; analytical workloads need consolidated historical data and efficient aggregation. The structure of the source data then influences whether it belongs in an operational database, warehouse, lake, or a combination. Finally, data modeling turns business concepts into entities, relationships, tables, keys, facts, and dimensions.

Business requirements | +-- Frequent inserts/updates, current state | -> OLTP operational database | +-- Historical analysis, aggregation, reporting | -> OLAP data warehouse / dimensional model | +-- Raw structured + semi-structured + unstructured data -> Data lake, then curate for analysis Sources -> Extract -> Transform -> Load -> Warehouse -> BI / Analytics Sources -> Extract -> Load -> Transform -> Curated analytical tables

OLTP and OLAP Workloads

OLTP supports current operational transactions; OLAP supports historical reporting and analysis. They solve different problems and commonly work together.

Data Structure, Storage, and Pipelines

Structured, semi-structured, and unstructured data have different schema behavior. Warehouses optimize curated analytics; lakes retain broad raw data at lower cost.

Data Modeling and Dimensional Design

Conceptual models describe entities and relationships. Logical models define tables and columns. Dimensional models organize measurable facts around descriptive dimensions.

Business Requirements First

There is no universally best database. The correct design follows workload, scale, latency, consistency, users, governance, and analytical requirements.

Section punchline: Choose the workload first, the storage pattern second, and the table design third. Technology should follow the questions and operations the business must support.

2. OLTP and OLAP Workloads

Online Transaction Processing (OLTP) systems support day-to-day business activity: placing an order, updating a customer record, recording employee hours, or checking a current price. They are application-oriented, hold current operational data, and favor many short, predictable queries with frequent inserts and updates. Strong integrity and concurrency matter because multiple users may change related records at the same time.

Online Analytical Processing (OLAP) systems support reporting and decision-making: calculating profit margin, identifying loyal customers, comparing periods, or finding trends. They are subject-oriented, consolidate data from multiple sources, preserve history, and favor complex scans and aggregations. OLTP and OLAP are complementary: operational systems capture business events; analytical systems summarize those events and feed insights back into business decisions.

-- OLTP: small, selective lookup of current state
SELECT price
FROM books
WHERE book_id = 42;

-- OLAP: scan and aggregate history by business dimension
SELECT
    customer_id,
    SUM(net_amount) AS lifetime_value
FROM fact_sales
GROUP BY customer_id
ORDER BY lifetime_value DESC;
What to notice: The SQL syntax is not what makes a system OLTP or OLAP. The workload pattern, data shape, update frequency, latency expectations, and optimization strategy do.

3. Data Structure, Storage, and Pipelines

Structured data follows a declared schema with defined types and relationships, such as relational tables. Semi-structured data carries self-describing markers but may not fit a fixed table shape, such as JSON or XML. Unstructured data includes images, audio, video, and free text without a tabular schema.

A traditional relational database is a natural fit for real-time structured operational data. A data warehouse is optimized for OLAP: integrated data, historical retention, read-heavy access, massively parallel processing, and often denormalized dimensional models. A data lake can retain raw structured, semi-structured, and unstructured data cheaply and apply schema on read, but it needs cataloging, quality controls, ownership, and lifecycle rules to avoid becoming a data swamp.

With ETL, transformation occurs before loading into the target warehouse. With ELT, data is loaded first and transformed inside a scalable analytical platform. The choice depends on target capability, governance, latency, data volume, and how much raw history must be retained.

-- Structured relational record
CREATE TABLE orders (
    order_id     BIGINT PRIMARY KEY,
    customer_id  BIGINT NOT NULL,
    ordered_at   TIMESTAMP NOT NULL,
    total_amount NUMERIC(12,2) NOT NULL
);

-- Semi-structured payload retained for flexible attributes
CREATE TABLE raw_events (
    event_id   BIGINT PRIMARY KEY,
    received_at TIMESTAMP NOT NULL,
    payload    JSONB NOT NULL
);
Common trap: A lake is not automatically better because it stores every data type, and a warehouse is not automatically obsolete. Storage flexibility without metadata, quality, security, and discoverability creates unusable data.

4. Data Modeling and Dimensional Design

Data modeling is the process of creating a usable representation of the data a system must store. A conceptual model captures business entities, attributes, and relationships without implementation detail. A logical model translates those ideas into tables, columns, keys, and relationships while remaining largely independent of a specific database engine. A physical model later adds engine-specific details such as data types, indexes, partitions, and storage choices.

For analytics, dimensional modeling adapts relational design around a business process. A fact table records measurable events at a declared grain, such as one row per sold line item. It changes frequently and links to dimensions through foreign keys. Dimension tables describe the entities used to filter, group, and label those facts, such as customer, product, artist, date, or store. The star schema keeps dimensions directly connected to the central fact table for understandable and efficient analytical queries.

Model / table type Primary purpose Key design question
Conceptual model Describe business entities, attributes, and relationships. What exists in the business, and how is it related?
Logical / dimensional model Define tables, columns, keys, fact grain, and dimensions. What is measured, at what grain, and by which descriptive dimensions?

5. Worked Example

Suppose a music business wants to analyze song performance by artist, album, genre, and date. The first design decision is the grain: one row in the fact table represents one song play. The play count and listening duration are measures; song, artist, album, genre, user, and date are dimensions or dimension attributes.

-- Central measurable event
CREATE TABLE fact_song_play (
    play_id       BIGINT PRIMARY KEY,
    date_key      INTEGER NOT NULL,
    song_key      INTEGER NOT NULL,
    user_key      INTEGER NOT NULL,
    play_count    INTEGER NOT NULL DEFAULT 1,
    seconds_played INTEGER NOT NULL
);

-- Descriptive dimension
CREATE TABLE dim_song (
    song_key      INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
    song_title    TEXT NOT NULL,
    album_title   TEXT,
    artist_name   TEXT NOT NULL,
    genre_name    TEXT
);

-- Analytical question: listening time by artist
SELECT
    s.artist_name,
    SUM(f.seconds_played) AS total_seconds
FROM fact_song_play AS f
JOIN dim_song AS s
  ON f.song_key = s.song_key
GROUP BY s.artist_name
ORDER BY total_seconds DESC;
Result: The design makes the analytical question direct: the fact table supplies additive measures, while the dimension supplies human-readable grouping attributes. Declaring the grain prevents ambiguous measures and accidental double-counting.

6. Validation and Troubleshooting

  • Write the main workload in plain English: transaction capture, operational lookup, historical analysis, or a combination.
  • Declare the grain of every fact table before selecting measures or dimensions.
  • Check whether each attribute is a measurable event, a descriptive property, or an identifier/key.
  • Confirm that storage choice matches data structure, latency, update pattern, scale, governance, and query behavior.
Red flag: A design is being driven by a fashionable technology name while the business questions, update patterns, fact grain, retention needs, and data consumers remain undefined.

7. Common Mistakes

Treating OLTP and OLAP as competing winners They serve different workloads. A healthy architecture often uses both: OLTP captures reliable operational events, then pipelines feed an OLAP platform for historical analysis.
Choosing storage only from the source file type Data format matters, but so do access pattern, latency, scale, cost, governance, retention, consistency, and downstream users. JSON can live in a relational database; structured data can also live in a lake.
Building a fact table before declaring its grain Without a one-sentence grain statement, measures from different levels can be mixed and joins can multiply rows. State exactly what one fact row represents first.

8. Interview Translation

How do OLTP and OLAP differ? OLTP systems optimize frequent, short, concurrent transactions against current operational data. OLAP systems optimize read-heavy scans, joins, and aggregations across consolidated historical data. OLTP captures the business; OLAP analyzes it.
When would you choose a warehouse instead of a data lake? I would favor a warehouse for governed, curated, high-performance BI and repeatable analytical workloads. I would favor a lake for low-cost retention of broad raw data, including semi-structured and unstructured sources. Many platforms use both as layered architecture.
What is the difference between a fact and a dimension? A fact table records a business event at a declared grain and stores measures plus foreign keys. Dimension tables describe the entities used to filter, group, and label those events. Facts answer how much or how many; dimensions answer who, what, where, when, and how.

9. Memory Review

Section summary
  1. OLTP runs the business; OLAP explains the business.
  2. Warehouse means curated analytical structure; lake means broad raw retention with schema commonly applied later.
  3. Conceptual modeling identifies entities and relationships; logical modeling defines tables, columns, and keys.
  4. For dimensional design: declare the business process and fact grain, then choose measures and dimensions.

Quick self-check

  • Can you classify “update the latest customer transaction” and “find the most loyal customers” as OLTP or OLAP and explain why?
  • Can you explain structured, semi-structured, and unstructured data without relying only on file extensions?
  • Can you state one practical difference among ETL, ELT, a warehouse, and a lake?
  • Can you design a simple star schema and state the exact grain of its fact table?
Lab decision: A focused local design lab is worthwhile. Model one operational source and one analytical star schema, load a small dataset, and run queries that prove the fact grain and dimension joins. A large infrastructure build is not necessary for Chapter 1.
Completion standard: Chapter 1 is complete when you can justify OLTP versus OLAP, classify storage patterns, explain ETL versus ELT, move from a conceptual model to tables, and identify fact grain, measures, and dimensions without guessing.
