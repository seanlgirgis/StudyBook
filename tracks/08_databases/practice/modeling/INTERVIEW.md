# Interview Questions — Data Modeling

> Topics covered: fact vs dimension · star schema · snowflake schema · SCD Type 1 · SCD Type 2 · data vault
> Levels: Starter | Mid | Senior | Architect

---

## Fact vs Dimension

### Starter

**Q1: In c001_fact_vs_dimension_demo.py, FACT_SALES holds `amount` while DIM_CUSTOMER holds `segment`. Why are they in separate tables?**
What a good answer covers:
- Fact tables store measurable, numeric events that occur repeatedly
- Dimension tables store descriptive attributes that give context to those events
- Separating them avoids repeating customer metadata on every sale row
Why this is asked: tests whether the candidate understands the foundational split between measurements and context.

**Q2: What makes a column a good candidate for a fact table versus a dimension table?**
What a good answer covers:
- Facts are typically additive numbers (amounts, quantities, durations) generated by an event
- Dimensions are descriptive text or categorical values that rarely change
- The grain of the fact table (one row = one sale) determines which columns belong where
Why this is asked: reveals whether the candidate can classify columns during design rather than by rote rule.

**Q3: Why does c001_fact_vs_dimension_demo.py use foreign keys like `customer_id` and `product_id` in FACT_SALES instead of embedding the customer name and product name directly?**
What a good answer covers:
- Foreign keys keep the fact table narrow and fast to scan
- Dimension attributes can be updated in one place without touching millions of fact rows
- Joining at query time is standard in dimensional models
Why this is asked: checks basic normalization awareness in the context of a star schema.

**Q4: What is the "grain" of a fact table, and why must it be defined before adding columns?**
What a good answer covers:
- Grain is the precise definition of what one row represents (e.g., one line item on one order)
- Every column added must be at that grain or the data becomes ambiguous
- Mixing grains causes double-counting and incorrect aggregations
Why this is asked: grain definition is the most common design mistake junior analysts make.

### Mid

**Q5: c001_fact_vs_dimension_demo.py stores `amount` as a single column. How would you handle a fact table that needs both pre-tax and post-tax amounts, and should these be stored or derived?**
What a good answer covers:
- Both can be stored as separate additive measures if queries frequently aggregate each independently
- Derived columns (post-tax = amount * (1 + rate)) save storage but cost CPU at query time
- For columnar warehouses, storing both is usually preferred for query performance
Why this is asked: probes the trade-off between storage cost and query-time computation.

**Q6: What is a factless fact table, and when would you use one?**
What a good answer covers:
- A factless fact table records that an event occurred but has no numeric measures
- Common for attendance, eligibility, or promotional coverage events
- Enables queries like "which products were promoted but not sold this week?"
Why this is asked: tests knowledge of edge cases in fact table design beyond simple sales examples.

**Q7: How do degenerate dimensions work, and what is an example from a sales context like c001?**
What a good answer covers:
- A degenerate dimension is a dimensional attribute stored directly on the fact row with no separate dimension table
- Order number or invoice number are classic examples — they have no additional attributes to store
- They serve as identifiers for grouping fact rows without needing a join
Why this is asked: distinguishes candidates who understand nuance from those who apply rules mechanically.

**Q8: What is the difference between additive, semi-additive, and non-additive measures? Give one example of each.**
What a good answer covers:
- Additive: can be summed across all dimensions (sales amount)
- Semi-additive: can be summed across some dimensions but not time (account balance at end of day)
- Non-additive: cannot be summed meaningfully in any direction (ratios, percentages, unit price)
Why this is asked: incorrect aggregation of semi-additive or non-additive facts is a common reporting bug.

### Senior

**Q9: A new requirement asks you to track sales at both the order level and the line-item level. How do you design fact tables to handle multiple grains without mixing them?**
What a good answer covers:
- Create two separate fact tables, each with a clearly defined grain
- Never combine two grains in one table — it forces artificial duplication or null columns
- Bridge or summary tables can roll up line-item facts to order-level for convenience
Why this is asked: multi-grain design is a real problem in growing data warehouses and reveals architectural thinking.

**Q10: How do you handle late-arriving facts in a warehouse built on top of a model like c001_fact_vs_dimension_demo.py?**
What a good answer covers:
- Late facts must be inserted at their correct event timestamp, not the processing timestamp
- Downstream aggregations or pre-built summaries may need to be invalidated and recomputed
- Partitioning by event date and tracking a separate load timestamp helps identify late data
Why this is asked: late data is inevitable in production and tests whether the candidate has operated real pipelines.

**Q11: When would you denormalize a dimension attribute directly onto a fact table even though it increases storage?**
What a good answer covers:
- When the attribute is queried as a filter or group-by on nearly every query against that fact
- When the join to the dimension is too expensive at the scale of the fact table
- When the attribute is immutable at the time of the event (e.g., the price at the moment of sale)
Why this is asked: reveals whether the candidate can weigh normalization principles against real query patterns.

### Architect

**Q12: You are designing a warehouse where the same logical fact (a payment) needs to be analyzed by finance at daily grain and by the fraud team at transaction grain. How do you structure the model to serve both without duplicating raw data?**
What a good answer covers:
- Raw fact table at transaction grain serves as the single source of truth
- A derived aggregate table at daily grain is built downstream for finance reporting
- Separate access layers (views or materialized tables) encapsulate each grain's logic
- Lineage tooling makes the relationship between layers explicit and auditable
Why this is asked: tests the ability to design layered models that serve multiple stakeholders without sacrificing consistency.

**Q13: How does the choice between a fact-and-dimension model and an event-sourcing approach affect how analytics consumers write queries?**
What a good answer covers:
- Dimensional models optimize for slice-and-dice aggregation with well-known join patterns
- Event sourcing stores immutable events in append-only logs, requiring replay or window functions to reconstruct state
- Analytical consumers generally prefer dimensional models; operational consumers often prefer event logs
- Hybrid architectures (lakehouse with both layers) are common in large organizations
Why this is asked: architects must choose storage patterns for both analytical and operational workloads and communicate trade-offs clearly.

## Star Schema

### Starter

**Q1: What is a star schema, and why is it called that?**
What a good answer covers:
- A central fact table surrounded by directly joined dimension tables
- The diagram resembles a star with the fact table at the center
- Designed to minimize the number of joins needed for typical analytical queries
Why this is asked: tests basic vocabulary before moving to design decisions.

**Q2: In a star schema for retail sales, name the likely fact table and at least three dimension tables.**
What a good answer covers:
- Fact: fact_sales with measures such as quantity, revenue, discount
- Dimensions: dim_date, dim_customer, dim_product, dim_store
- Each dimension connects to the fact via a surrogate key
Why this is asked: forces the candidate to apply the concept to a concrete domain.

**Q3: What is a surrogate key, and why use one instead of the natural business key in a star schema?**
What a good answer covers:
- A surrogate key is a system-generated integer with no business meaning
- Natural keys can change, be reused, or contain encoding that couples the model to source systems
- Surrogate keys are stable, compact, and support SCD patterns without changing fact rows
Why this is asked: surrogate vs natural key is a daily design decision in dimensional modeling.

**Q4: What are the advantages of a star schema for BI tools and analysts?**
What a good answer covers:
- Single join from fact to each dimension — easy for non-technical users to understand
- BI tools can auto-generate queries from metadata without complex path resolution
- Aggregations are fast because dimension filters collapse to a key lookup before scanning facts
Why this is asked: connects design choices to end-user experience, which is often overlooked.

### Mid

**Q5: How does a star schema handle a dimension that is shared by multiple fact tables, such as a date dimension?**
What a good answer covers:
- The shared dimension table is joined to each fact table independently — this is called a conformed dimension
- Conformed dimensions allow cross-fact drill-through (comparing sales to returns by month)
- The dimension definition and keys must be identical across both fact tables
Why this is asked: conformed dimensions are the mechanism that makes a data warehouse coherent across subject areas.

**Q6: What is a junk dimension, and when does it help a star schema?**
What a good answer covers:
- A junk dimension bundles low-cardinality flags and indicators (e.g., is_promo, is_online, is_return) into one table
- Avoids cluttering the fact table with many boolean columns or creating dozens of tiny dimension tables
- The combinatorial explosion of flags is pre-materialized so the dimension stays small
Why this is asked: junk dimensions are a practical design pattern that tests real-world modeling experience.

**Q7: How does query performance differ between a star schema and a highly normalized (3NF) model on the same data?**
What a good answer covers:
- Star schema requires fewer joins (one per dimension) versus many joins through a normalized chain
- Columnar warehouses benefit further because dimension filters reduce fact scans early
- 3NF is optimal for OLTP write performance but slow for analytical aggregation
Why this is asked: tests whether the candidate understands why OLAP models deliberately denormalize.

**Q8: What risks arise when a fact table's grain is broader than the lowest level at which a dimension is defined?**
What a good answer covers:
- The join inflates rows (fan-out) and causes double-counting in aggregations
- This is the "chasm trap" — a classic modeling bug
- The solution is to define consistent grain or aggregate the dimension before joining
Why this is asked: chasm traps cause silent data errors that are hard to detect after deployment.

### Senior

**Q9: You have a star schema with a 2-billion-row fact table. A business user adds a new dimension attribute mid-year and wants it applied to historical facts. How do you handle this?**
What a good answer covers:
- If the attribute is on the dimension (not the fact), update the dimension table without touching the fact table
- If it must be on the fact (e.g., a category valid at event time), decide whether to backfill or treat pre-change rows as null/unknown
- Large backfills on fact tables require careful partition management and downstream cache invalidation
Why this is asked: reveals operational experience with schema evolution in production warehouses.

**Q10: How do you handle many-to-many relationships between a fact table and a dimension in a star schema?**
What a good answer covers:
- Use a bridge table that sits between the fact and the dimension
- The bridge holds one row per fact-dimension combination with an optional weighting factor
- BI tools need special handling for bridge tables to avoid double-counting
Why this is asked: many-to-many relationships (e.g., orders with multiple promotions) are common and often mishandled.

**Q11: Describe how you would design a star schema to support time-zone-aware reporting across a global user base.**
What a good answer covers:
- Store all timestamps in UTC on the fact table and include a UTC date foreign key
- Add a local-time date key derived from the user's or store's timezone for local reporting
- The date dimension may need two sets of date attributes, or a separate timezone dimension
Why this is asked: timezone handling is a recurring production problem that exposes depth of experience.

### Architect

**Q12: When would you recommend a star schema over a data vault as the primary storage layer, and what would drive the opposite recommendation?**
What a good answer covers:
- Star schema: team prioritizes query simplicity, source systems are stable, and the model is well understood in advance
- Data vault: sources change frequently, auditability of raw history is required, or multiple source systems contribute to the same entities
- Many teams combine both: data vault as the integration layer, star schema as the consumption layer
Why this is asked: forces the candidate to reason about architecture selection criteria rather than defaulting to one approach.

**Q13: How does the star schema pattern need to evolve to support a real-time analytics use case where facts arrive within seconds of the event?**
What a good answer covers:
- Streaming ingestion replaces batch ETL for the fact table, requiring the dimension keys to be resolved at ingest time or via late-join enrichment
- Micro-batch or lambda architectures maintain a hot layer (recent streaming data) and a cold layer (historical batch data)
- Surrogate key resolution becomes a bottleneck if dimensions are not cached or pre-loaded
- SLAs for dimension freshness must be defined independently of fact freshness
Why this is asked: tests whether the candidate can extend a well-known batch pattern to a streaming world.

## Snowflake Schema

### Starter

**Q1: How does a snowflake schema differ from a star schema?**
What a good answer covers:
- Dimension tables are further normalized into sub-dimension tables (e.g., dim_product references dim_category)
- Reduces redundant data in dimensions but requires more joins to reach leaf attributes
- The diagram looks like a snowflake because of the branching dimension chains
Why this is asked: ensures the candidate can distinguish the two most common OLAP schema patterns.

**Q2: Give an example of a dimension hierarchy that would motivate normalizing a dimension into a snowflake structure.**
What a good answer covers:
- Product -> Category -> Department: category and department attributes repeat for every product
- Geography: city -> state -> country stored in separate tables
- Normalization removes repeated values but adds joins
Why this is asked: grounds the abstract concept in a real hierarchy.

**Q3: What are the storage trade-offs of snowflake versus star schema?**
What a good answer covers:
- Snowflake stores less duplicate data in dimensions — beneficial for very large, high-cardinality dimensions
- Star schema stores denormalized dimensions that are larger but resolve in one join
- For typical dimension sizes (thousands to millions of rows), the storage difference is often negligible
Why this is asked: candidates should be able to state the trade-off without overstating the benefit.

**Q4: When is a snowflake schema likely to hurt query performance compared to a star schema?**
What a good answer covers:
- Each level of normalization adds a join, increasing query plan complexity
- BI tools that auto-generate SQL may produce inefficient multi-join queries
- For small to medium dimensions, denormalizing back to star is almost always faster on modern columnar engines
Why this is asked: prevents the misconception that more normalization is always better.

### Mid

**Q5: How do you decide which dimensions to normalize in a snowflake versus which to leave denormalized?**
What a good answer covers:
- Normalize when the sub-dimension is large and shared across multiple parent dimensions
- Leave flat when the dimension is small (under a few million rows) and the join cost exceeds the storage savings
- Consider whether downstream BI tools handle multi-hop joins gracefully
Why this is asked: design decisions require judgment, not just pattern memorization.

**Q6: In c003_snowflake_schema_demo.py, a product dimension references a separate category table. How does this affect ETL when a category is renamed?**
What a good answer covers:
- Only the category row needs updating; all products in that category reflect the change immediately
- In a star schema, every product row storing the category name would require an update
- The trade-off is that ETL must maintain referential integrity across tables
Why this is asked: connects schema structure to ETL maintenance cost.

**Q7: What is a role-playing dimension, and can it exist in a snowflake schema?**
What a good answer covers:
- A role-playing dimension is a single physical dimension used multiple times in one fact table under different aliases (e.g., order_date, ship_date, delivery_date all referencing dim_date)
- It works in both star and snowflake schemas — aliases are implemented as views
- Snowflake schemas complicate role-playing slightly if the aliased dimension has normalized sub-tables
Why this is asked: role-playing dimensions test nuanced understanding of shared dimensions.

**Q8: How does a snowflake schema interact with slowly changing dimensions?**
What a good answer covers:
- SCD logic applies at each level of the snowflake independently
- A category rename (SCD Type 1 at the category level) affects all products in that category
- An SCD Type 2 at the product level adds a new product row but the category join must still resolve correctly for historical periods
Why this is asked: tests integration of two important modeling concepts.

### Senior

**Q9: A BI team reports slow dashboard load times on a snowflake schema. What is your diagnostic process and likely solutions?**
What a good answer covers:
- Profile query plans to identify which joins are most expensive
- Check whether dimension sub-tables have appropriate indexes or distribution keys
- Consider flattening the most frequently traversed dimension chains into denormalized views
- Materialized views or pre-aggregated summary tables can absorb the join cost for common queries
Why this is asked: performance tuning requires systematic diagnosis, not guesswork.

**Q10: How would you migrate an existing star schema to a snowflake schema without breaking existing BI reports?**
What a good answer covers:
- Create the new normalized sub-tables and populate them from the existing dimension data
- Rebuild the original dimension table as a view that joins the sub-tables, preserving the old column interface
- Test BI reports against the view before switching to direct snowflake joins
- Deprecate the view only after all consumers have been updated
Why this is asked: migration without breaking consumers is a real architectural challenge.

**Q11: What are the implications of a snowflake schema for a data catalog or semantic layer tool?**
What a good answer covers:
- Semantic layers must model the multi-hop join paths explicitly so end users see a flat logical model
- Lineage graphs become more complex with additional nodes per dimension hierarchy
- Governance tools need to track attribute definitions at each level of the hierarchy
Why this is asked: modern data stacks layer semantic tools on top of physical schemas, and architects must account for this.

### Architect

**Q12: At what data scale and team maturity level would you recommend starting with a snowflake schema versus defaulting to a star schema?**
What a good answer covers:
- Star schema is almost always the better starting point — simpler to build, query, and explain
- Snowflake schema pays off when dimensions are very large and shared across many fact tables in a mature, well-governed warehouse
- Team SQL fluency and BI tool compatibility matter more than storage savings at most scales
Why this is asked: tests whether the candidate defaults to complexity or defaults to simplicity.

**Q13: How does the snowflake schema pattern interact with a lakehouse architecture where storage is object-based and there is no traditional query optimizer?**
What a good answer covers:
- Without pushed-down join optimization, multi-hop snowflake joins can be very expensive on object storage
- Denormalizing into wide Parquet or Delta tables is often preferred in lakehouse environments
- The semantic layer absorbs the complexity that the schema used to encode, keeping physical data flat
- Partitioning strategies replace the performance role of normalization
Why this is asked: lakehouse adoption has changed when snowflake schemas are appropriate, and architects must adapt.

## SCD Type 1

### Starter

**Q1: What does SCD stand for, and what does Type 1 do to a dimension row when an attribute changes?**
What a good answer covers:
- SCD stands for Slowly Changing Dimension
- Type 1 overwrites the existing row with the new value — no history is kept
- The old value is permanently lost after the update
Why this is asked: baseline vocabulary for dimension history management.

**Q2: Give an example of a customer attribute that is appropriate to manage with SCD Type 1.**
What a good answer covers:
- Corrected spelling of a customer name (the old spelling was an error, not a real state)
- Email address or phone number where only the current value matters for operations
- Any attribute where the business explicitly does not need historical values
Why this is asked: the best SCD type depends on business requirements, not just technical convention.

**Q3: How does SCD Type 1 affect downstream reports that were run before the change?**
What a good answer covers:
- Historical reports that referenced the old attribute value will now show the new value if re-run
- There is no way to recover the old value from the dimension table alone
- This is acceptable when the change is a correction but problematic when it was a real business change
Why this is asked: highlights the business impact of overwriting history.

**Q4: What SQL operation implements SCD Type 1, and what key does it match on?**
What a good answer covers:
- An UPDATE statement targeting the existing row in the dimension table
- Matching is done on the natural or surrogate key that uniquely identifies the entity
- The new column values overwrite the old ones in place
Why this is asked: connects concept to implementation.

### Mid

**Q5: How do you implement SCD Type 1 as part of a daily pipeline without re-scanning the entire dimension table?**
What a good answer covers:
- Track a `last_modified` timestamp on source records and filter the CDC feed for changed rows
- Use MERGE (UPSERT) to update only changed rows and insert new rows in one statement
- Maintain a high-watermark or CDC log position to avoid re-processing unchanged records
Why this is asked: efficiency matters at scale — full scans on large dimensions are expensive.

**Q6: A data engineer applies SCD Type 1 to a `customer_segment` column that the business later decides should have been tracked historically. What is the recovery path?**
What a good answer covers:
- If source system history is available, backfill by replaying historical change events
- If no source history exists, the historical dimension values are unrecoverable from the warehouse alone
- Going forward, switch to SCD Type 2 and accept that pre-change history will be unknown
Why this is asked: design mistakes happen; the candidate should know how to recover and prevent recurrence.

**Q7: How does SCD Type 1 interact with a fact table that references the dimension via surrogate key?**
What a good answer covers:
- The surrogate key on the fact row does not change — the fact still points to the same dimension row
- After a Type 1 update, all historical facts now appear to have the new attribute value
- This is correct behavior when the update is a data correction, but misleading for genuine business changes
Why this is asked: understanding the join behavior explains why Type 1 is appropriate only for specific use cases.

**Q8: When would you apply SCD Type 1 on top of a SCD Type 2 design for the same dimension?**
What a good answer covers:
- Some attributes in a dimension need history (Type 2) while others do not (Type 1 on all versions)
- For example, a customer's loyalty tier is tracked historically (Type 2), but a corrected postal code is overwritten on all rows (Type 1 applied across versions)
- This hybrid approach requires deciding per-attribute which strategy applies
Why this is asked: real dimensions often mix SCD strategies, and this tests depth of understanding.

### Senior

**Q9: Design a MERGE statement strategy for SCD Type 1 that handles inserts, updates, and deletes in a single pass.**
What a good answer covers:
- MERGE matches on the natural key; WHEN MATCHED AND changed → UPDATE; WHEN NOT MATCHED → INSERT
- Deletes can be handled as soft-deletes by setting an `is_active` flag rather than physical deletion
- Change detection in the WHEN MATCHED clause should compare only meaningful columns to avoid unnecessary writes
Why this is asked: efficient SCD Type 1 implementation is a core data engineering skill.

**Q10: How do you handle SCD Type 1 in a streaming pipeline where dimension updates and fact events may arrive out of order?**
What a good answer covers:
- Buffer incoming dimension updates and apply them before enriching facts with dimension attributes
- Use a state store (e.g., Redis or RocksDB in a stream processor) to hold the current dimension snapshot
- Accept that facts arriving before their dimension update may be enriched with stale attributes and decide whether to reprocess them
Why this is asked: streaming pipelines require different SCD handling than batch pipelines.

**Q11: What audit or lineage information should you capture alongside a SCD Type 1 update to support data governance requirements?**
What a good answer covers:
- Record `updated_at` timestamp, `updated_by` (pipeline or user identity), and the source system event ID
- Log the old and new values in a separate audit table if full history of changes is needed for compliance
- Lineage tools should record that the dimension row was modified and by which pipeline run
Why this is asked: governance requirements often extend beyond what the dimension table itself captures.

### Architect

**Q12: A regulatory requirement mandates that all changes to customer data be auditable for seven years, but the current pipeline uses SCD Type 1. How do you redesign the architecture?**
What a good answer covers:
- Add an audit log table that captures every pre-change and post-change state with timestamps and actor identity
- Alternatively, migrate dimension management to SCD Type 2 and retain all historical rows for the retention period
- The audit log approach keeps the dimension simple while satisfying compliance separately
- Data retention policies and purge automation must also be designed for the seven-year window
Why this is asked: compliance requirements regularly force architects to revisit seemingly simple design choices.

**Q13: How does the SCD Type 1 pattern need to change when the dimension is shared across a federated multi-cloud data mesh?**
What a good answer covers:
- A centralized dimension update must propagate to all domain data products that own a local copy
- Change data capture events from the authoritative domain must be subscribed to by consuming domains
- Each domain decides independently when to apply the update, creating temporary inconsistency windows
- Contracts and SLAs for propagation latency must be defined at the mesh governance level
Why this is asked: data mesh introduces distributed ownership that challenges centralized SCD patterns.

## SCD Type 2

### Starter

**Q1: Looking at c005_scd_type2_demo.py, customer C1 appears twice in DIM_CUSTOMER with different `segment` values and different `effective_start` dates. Why are there two rows instead of one updated row?**
What a good answer covers:
- SCD Type 2 preserves history by inserting a new row for each change rather than overwriting
- The old row is closed (effective_end set, current_flag set to False) and a new row is opened
- Facts joined to the dimension can retrieve the segment that was current at the time of the sale
Why this is asked: directly tests whether the candidate can read and explain the core SCD Type 2 pattern.

**Q2: What do the `effective_start`, `effective_end`, and `current_flag` columns in c005_scd_type2_demo.py each represent?**
What a good answer covers:
- `effective_start`: the date the dimension row became the active version
- `effective_end`: the date the row was superseded (9999-12-31 or NULL indicates currently active)
- `current_flag`: a convenience boolean that quickly identifies the active row without a date comparison
Why this is asked: these three columns are the standard implementation of SCD Type 2 and must be understood precisely.

**Q3: How does a fact row join to the correct version of a SCD Type 2 dimension?**
What a good answer covers:
- Join on the natural key (customer_id) AND the condition that the fact's event date falls between effective_start and effective_end
- Alternatively, the fact row can store the surrogate key of the dimension version that was current at load time
- The surrogate key approach is faster at query time but requires key resolution during fact loading
Why this is asked: the join pattern is the most operationally important aspect of SCD Type 2.

**Q4: What happens to existing fact rows when a customer's segment changes and a new SCD Type 2 row is inserted?**
What a good answer covers:
- Existing fact rows that stored the old surrogate key continue to point to the old dimension version
- Queries using the date-range join pattern automatically resolve to the historically correct row
- No fact rows need to be updated — this is a key advantage of SCD Type 2
Why this is asked: ensures the candidate understands the immutability benefit of the pattern.

### Mid

**Q5: In c005_scd_type2_demo.py, sale_id 1 (date 2024-01-10) and sale_id 2 (date 2024-02-10) reference the same customer C1. Which dimension row should each sale join to, and why?**
What a good answer covers:
- Sale 1 (Jan 10) joins to the row with effective_start 2024-01-01 and effective_end 2024-01-31 (segment: consumer)
- Sale 2 (Feb 10) joins to the row with effective_start 2024-02-01 (segment: business)
- The join condition is: customer_id matches AND sale_date >= effective_start AND sale_date <= effective_end
Why this is asked: directly applies the demo data to test whether the candidate can trace a concrete query.

**Q6: How do you implement a SCD Type 2 update in SQL when a source record changes?**
What a good answer covers:
- Detect changed rows by comparing source attributes to the current dimension version
- UPDATE the existing current row: set effective_end to today minus one day, set current_flag to False
- INSERT a new row with effective_start as today, effective_end as 9999-12-31, current_flag as True
- Both operations should be atomic (wrapped in a transaction or a single MERGE statement)
Why this is asked: tests practical implementation knowledge beyond conceptual understanding.

**Q7: What are the storage and query performance implications of SCD Type 2 for a dimension with frequent changes?**
What a good answer covers:
- Dimension tables grow with every change — a customer who changes segment monthly accumulates 12 rows per year
- Date-range joins are more expensive than simple key lookups and can prevent index use on some engines
- Partitioning the dimension by current_flag or effective_end can speed up queries that only need the current snapshot
Why this is asked: SCD Type 2 has real costs that must be weighed against the business need for history.

**Q8: How do you handle the case where a source system sends a corrected historical record — for example, the customer's segment on 2024-01-01 was logged incorrectly?**
What a good answer covers:
- If only the attribute value is wrong (not the date range), update the affected historical row's attribute in place (a Type 1 correction on a Type 2 row)
- If the effective date was also wrong, the row must be closed and re-opened with the corrected dates, which may require adjusting adjacent rows
- Audit the correction with metadata (who corrected it, when, from what value) to maintain a trustworthy history
Why this is asked: corrections to historical records are common and test the candidate's understanding of history management complexity.

### Senior

**Q9: Design an idempotent SCD Type 2 load process that can be safely re-run if the pipeline fails halfway through.**
What a good answer covers:
- Use a staging table to isolate incoming changes from the live dimension
- Apply changes using a MERGE or two-step UPDATE/INSERT inside a transaction — either both commit or neither does
- Track a pipeline run ID on each dimension row to detect whether a re-run has already applied the changes
- Re-running should detect that the new row already exists and skip the insert, and that the old row is already closed and skip the update
Why this is asked: idempotency is a production requirement for any pipeline that must survive failures.

**Q10: How do you handle SCD Type 2 when two changes to the same entity arrive in the same pipeline batch?**
What a good answer covers:
- Sort the changes by their effective date before processing so versions are inserted in chronological order
- The first change closes the current row and opens version 2; the second change closes version 2 and opens version 3
- Processing in random order would produce overlapping or incorrect date ranges
Why this is asked: batch deduplication and ordering are a real implementation challenge.

**Q11: How would you restructure a SCD Type 2 dimension to support point-in-time snapshots efficiently at scale?**
What a good answer covers:
- Pre-materialize daily snapshots as a separate table (one row per entity per day) for fast point-in-time queries
- Use partition pruning on the snapshot date to make "what was the state on date X" queries efficient
- Accept higher storage cost in exchange for eliminating date-range joins at query time
- The snapshot table is rebuilt or incrementally appended by a scheduled pipeline from the SCD Type 2 table
Why this is asked: point-in-time snapshots are a common evolution of SCD Type 2 in high-volume warehouses.

### Architect

**Q12: A business unit wants to analyze customer behavior using the segment that was current at the time of each sale, while the compliance team wants to always see the current segment for regulatory reports. How do you architect the dimension and reporting layers to serve both without duplicating fact data?**
What a good answer covers:
- The SCD Type 2 dimension table supports historical analysis via date-range joins
- A current-snapshot view (filtering current_flag = True) serves compliance reports that always want present-day attributes
- The fact table stores the surrogate key resolved at load time, enabling the historical join without re-resolving dates
- The semantic or reporting layer exposes both join patterns as separate logical models labeled clearly for each team
Why this is asked: serving multiple consumers with different time semantics is a core data warehouse design challenge.

**Q13: How does SCD Type 2 interact with a streaming pipeline where dimension changes and facts may arrive within milliseconds of each other?**
What a good answer covers:
- The surrogate key on a fact must be resolved at ingest time against the dimension version current at that instant
- If the dimension update and the fact event arrive in the same micro-batch, processing order determines correctness
- A late-arriving dimension change after the fact has already been loaded requires retroactive re-keying of the fact or an alternative enrichment strategy
- Materialized state stores (e.g., a cache of current dimension rows) are used for low-latency key resolution rather than querying the warehouse dimension table
Why this is asked: streaming workloads expose race conditions inherent in SCD Type 2 that batch pipelines hide behind processing windows.

## Data Vault

### Starter

**Q1: What are the three core table types in a data vault model?**
What a good answer covers:
- Hub: stores the unique business keys for each core business entity (customer, product, order)
- Link: stores the relationships between hubs, with foreign keys to each hub's surrogate key
- Satellite: stores the descriptive attributes and context for hubs or links, with full history
Why this is asked: the three-table pattern is the foundational vocabulary of data vault.

**Q2: Why does a hub table in data vault contain only the business key and metadata — no descriptive attributes?**
What a good answer covers:
- Hubs are designed to be stable and never change after insert — only the business key identity matters
- Descriptive attributes go in satellites, which track history independently
- This separation means a new attribute can be added without altering the hub
Why this is asked: the separation of identity from context is the defining principle of data vault.

**Q3: What problem does data vault solve that a traditional star schema does not handle well?**
What a good answer covers:
- Data vault is designed for integrating data from many heterogeneous source systems over time
- It absorbs schema changes without breaking existing tables — new satellites are added, not existing tables altered
- It preserves raw source data with full auditability before any business rules are applied
Why this is asked: selecting the right modeling approach requires knowing what problem each solves.

**Q4: What is a load date and a record source in data vault, and why are they on every table?**
What a good answer covers:
- Load date: the timestamp when the record was loaded into the vault — provides audit trail of when data arrived
- Record source: identifies which source system provided the record — enables conflict resolution and lineage
- Together they support full data lineage and make the vault a reliable historical record
Why this is asked: these metadata columns are mandatory in data vault and distinguish it from other approaches.

### Mid

**Q5: How does a data vault satellite handle attribute history compared to SCD Type 2?**
What a good answer covers:
- Satellites append a new row for every detected change — similar to SCD Type 2 in concept
- Unlike SCD Type 2, there are no effective_end dates; the end of a period is derived by the next row's load_date
- Satellites can be split by rate of change (fast-changing vs slow-changing attributes in separate satellites) for efficiency
Why this is asked: the difference between satellite history and SCD Type 2 history is subtle but important.

**Q6: What is a link table in data vault, and how does it differ from a foreign key in a normalized model?**
What a good answer covers:
- A link captures a many-to-many or any-to-any relationship as a first-class table with its own surrogate key
- Links are insert-only and never updated — historical relationships are preserved
- In a normalized model a foreign key on one table enforces a one-to-many relationship; a link is more flexible and auditable
Why this is asked: links are the mechanism that makes data vault resilient to relationship cardinality changes.

**Q7: What is the difference between a raw vault and a business vault, and when are business rules applied?**
What a good answer covers:
- Raw vault: stores data exactly as received from source systems with no transformations — pure integration layer
- Business vault: applies business rules, interpretations, and derived attributes on top of the raw vault
- Business rules are kept out of the raw vault to preserve an unmodified audit record and to allow rule changes without re-loading source data
Why this is asked: the raw/business vault split is a critical governance and auditability design decision.

**Q8: How do you handle a business key that changes in the source system in a data vault model?**
What a good answer covers:
- A new hub row is inserted for the new key — the old row remains unchanged
- A satellite can record the key change event with appropriate metadata
- A same-as link (alias link) can be used to connect the old and new hub rows as representing the same entity
Why this is asked: business key changes are a real operational problem that data vault has a specific pattern to address.

### Senior

**Q9: Describe the end-to-end flow from a source system change to a consumable report in a data vault architecture.**
What a good answer covers:
- CDC or batch extract captures changed records from the source system
- Records are loaded into hubs (new business keys), links (new relationships), and satellites (new attribute snapshots) in the raw vault
- Business vault applies transformations and derived columns as a separate layer
- Information marts (star schema or wide tables) are built from the business vault for BI consumption
- Each layer has clear lineage back to the source record and load timestamp
Why this is asked: end-to-end flow tests architectural understanding, not just table-level knowledge.

**Q10: How does data vault handle parallel loading from multiple source systems that contribute data about the same business entity?**
What a good answer covers:
- Each source system inserts its own satellite rows with its own record source tag — no merging at load time
- The hub receives inserts from all sources; the business key deduplication prevents duplicate hub rows
- Conflict resolution between sources happens in the business vault layer, not in the raw vault
Why this is asked: multi-source integration is data vault's primary use case and tests real architectural understanding.

**Q11: What are the performance challenges of querying a data vault directly, and how do information marts address them?**
What a good answer covers:
- Raw vault queries require joining hubs, links, and multiple satellites — complex and slow for BI tools
- Satellite history rows require filtering to the latest or point-in-time version before joining
- Information marts pre-join and pre-aggregate vault data into star schema or wide tables for fast BI consumption
- The mart rebuild pipeline must be designed for incremental updates to avoid full re-scans
Why this is asked: data vault is rarely queried directly in production; understanding the mart layer is essential.

### Architect

**Q12: A team is choosing between data vault and a medallion lakehouse architecture (bronze/silver/gold). How do you frame the trade-offs to help them decide?**
What a good answer covers:
- Data vault: strong auditability, standardized patterns, good for regulated industries with many source systems
- Medallion: more flexible, fits naturally into Spark/Delta Lake ecosystems, less prescriptive on table design
- Both separate raw ingestion from business logic — the key difference is the level of structural prescription
- Team skill set, regulatory requirements, and tooling ecosystem should drive the decision
Why this is asked: architects must compare approaches without bias toward a single methodology.

**Q13: How would you evolve a data vault architecture to support near-real-time data products while maintaining the auditability guarantees of the raw vault?**
What a good answer covers:
- Replace batch CDC with streaming ingestion (e.g., Kafka) feeding a streaming load process into hubs, links, and satellites
- The raw vault remains insert-only and append-only — streaming does not change this constraint
- Business vault and information marts are updated incrementally via micro-batch or streaming materialization
- The load_date on each record provides the audit trail regardless of whether loading is batch or streaming
Why this is asked: real-time requirements are now common even in governance-heavy environments, and architects must know how to adapt vault patterns without abandoning their auditability guarantees.
